New founders often price low. It feels kind, it feels competitive, and it seems to make the first sale easier. The trouble shows up later, in a number most people never work out: whether a customer ever pays back what it cost to win them.

## Two ways to set a price

**Cost-plus:** add up what it costs you to serve a customer and add a margin. It's simple, and it guarantees you're not selling at a loss on each unit.

**Value-based:** start from what the problem costs the customer. A shop owner who lost most of a weekend's orders to an outage isn't comparing your price with your server bill. They're comparing it with that weekend. Cost tells you the lowest price that makes sense; value tells you how high you can go.

Whichever you start from, the price has to clear one test: does a customer bring in more than they cost?

## The unit economics of one customer

Andreessen Horowitz's *16 Startup Metrics* defines the pieces. In plain terms:

- **CAC (customer acquisition cost):** the full cost of winning one customer, including ads, referral fees, credits and discounts.
- **Gross profit per month:** price minus what it costs to serve that customer.
- **Churn:** the share of customers who cancel each month. The a16z post puts the expected lifetime, in months, at 1 divided by monthly churn.
- **LTV (lifetime value):** the profit from a customer over the relationship. The post's simple version is contribution margin multiplied by expected lifetime; here, gross profit per month stands in for contribution margin.
- **Payback:** how many months of gross profit it takes to earn back the CAC.

## Try it

The numbers below belong to this toy shop, not to any industry: $1.50 a month to serve each customer, $60 to win one, and 5% of monthly customers cancelling each month. Change the price and the billing, and watch payback move.

<Callout kind="trap">
At $3 a month every customer looks profitable: $3 in, $1.50 out. But at 5% churn the average customer stays 20 months and earns back only $30 of the $60 it took to win them. The business loses $30 on every new customer, so growing faster just loses money faster.
</Callout>

## Annual billing moves the cash forward

Offering a year upfront at a discount (here, a year for the price of ten months) changes when the money arrives. At $6 a month the annual payment is $60, which covers the cost of winning that customer on day one. That cash can fund winning the next customer, rather than waiting a year for each one to pay back.

<Callout kind="real">
Real numbers come from your own data, and early on you won't have much: a few customers, a guessed churn rate. That's fine. The habit that matters is writing the formula down and putting your best numbers in it before you pick a price, so you know which assumption to check first.
</Callout>
