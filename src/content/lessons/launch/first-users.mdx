Launch day feels like the finish line. You post it somewhere big, the sign-ups roll in, and the number looks wonderful. A month later, most of those accounts were never used.

That's the difference between a launch metric and a product metric. **Sign-ups** measure how good your launch post was. Whether people **set it up** and are **still using it weeks later** measures whether your product solves their problem.

## Go where your users already are

A post on a big general tech site reaches a lot of people, and few of them run small online shops. A thread where shop owners already ask "why did my site go down?" reaches very few people, and almost all of them have the problem. Answering questions there honestly, and mentioning what you've built when it helps, brings fewer visitors and far better ones.

## Do things that don't scale

Paul Graham's essay of that name argues that at the start you should take extraordinary measures, both to acquire users and to make them happy. For this product, that could mean getting on a call with each new user and setting up their first check together. It takes half an hour per person. With 400 sign-ups that's impossible; with 45, it's the best use of your week.

## Measure what matters

The a16z metrics post describes measuring **retention by cohort**: take each original group of customers and track what percentage of them is still active as the months go by. It adds that investors want to see engagement "ideally expressed as cohort retention". The habit to build early is the same question on a small scale: of the people who signed up in a given week, how many are still active four weeks later? That tells you more than a running total of sign-ups.
