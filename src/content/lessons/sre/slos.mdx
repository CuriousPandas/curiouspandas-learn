"Never go down" sounds like the right goal. It's the wrong one. The SRE book says it plainly: "100% is probably never the right reliability target." Nobody can reach it, users can't tell 99.99% from 100% through their own flaky Wi-Fi, and every step closer costs more and slows every change.

So reliability work starts by choosing a number, and the number gets three names.

- An **SLI** (service level *indicator*) is "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service". For example: the share of checkout requests that succeed in under 500 ms.
- An **SLO** (service level *objective*) is "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI". For example: 99.9% of them, measured over 30 days.
- An **SLA** (service level *agreement*) is a contract with users "that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs". Miss it and someone pays. That's why an SLA is usually looser than the SLO the team works to.

## The budget

If the target is 99.9%, then 0.1% failure is allowed. That allowance is the **error budget**. Thirty days is 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes, and 0.1% of that is **43.2 minutes**. That's the budget for total outage in a month, or the same failure spread thinner: 86 minutes of half the requests failing spends it just as surely.

The budget turns an argument into arithmetic. Budget left: ship, experiment, take risks. Budget spent: the SRE book's answer is that "releases are temporarily halted" until reliability recovers. Nobody has to win a debate about whether the product is "stable enough".

## The alert is the hard part

A budget that nobody watches is a number in a slide deck. The obvious alert is "page when the error rate goes above 0.1%". The SRE Workbook uses exactly that as its first example, and explains why it fails: it "fires on any event that threatens the SLO", which is good, but its "precision is low: The alert fires on many events that do not threaten the SLO." Their example: "A 0.1% error rate for 10 minutes would alert, while consuming only 0.02% of the monthly error budget."

The opposite mistake is to average over a long window so blips vanish. Now a real fire takes so long to move the average that the budget is half gone before anyone is told.

The workbook's answer is the **burn rate**: "how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget." A burn rate of 1 spends the budget in exactly 30 days. A burn rate of 14.4 spends 2% of it in a single hour. Its recommended policy for a 99.9% SLO:

| Burn rate | Long window | Short window | Budget spent | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14.4 | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 2% | page |
| 6 | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 5% | page |
| 1 | 3 days | 6 hours | 10% | ticket |

The **long window** proves the burn is real, not a blip. The **short window** proves it is still happening, so the alert stops firing soon after the fire is out: "ceasing to fire five minutes later, rather than one hour later."

Try all three alerts against all three incidents. Only one gets every call right.

<Callout kind="trap">
A page is a person woken at 3 a.m. An alert that pages on every blip teaches people to silence alerts, and then the real one is silenced too. The slow leak needs fixing this week, not tonight. That is what a ticket is for.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="real">
Real SLOs start from what users notice: availability and latency on the path that matters (checkout, login, search), measured as close to the user as you can. Generators such as [Pyrra](https://pyrra.dev/) (which writes multi-window burn-rate alerts) and [Sloth](https://sloth.dev/) turn a short SLO definition into Prometheus rules, but no tool can choose the SLI. That part is a conversation with the people who use the service.
</Callout>

<DoItForReal id="sre-slo-real" profile="observability">
On the tilt platform, Prometheus scrapes Istio's request metrics (`istio_requests_total`), the same series lab 06's canary analysis reads. Send some traffic to hello, then in Grafana's Explore ([grafana.localhost/explore](https://grafana.localhost/explore)) try an availability SLI over the last hour:

```promql
sum(rate(istio_requests_total{destination_service_name="hello", response_code!~"5.."}[1h]))
/
sum(rate(istio_requests_total{destination_service_name="hello"}[1h]))
```

Subtract that from 1 and divide by `0.001` - that is, `(1 - SLI) / 0.001` - and you have the 1-hour burn rate for a 99.9% SLO. If the query returns nothing, check the label names first with `istio_requests_total{destination_service_name=~"hello.*"}`: labels differ between gateway and sidecar proxies, and an empty result is a missing match, not a perfect service.
</DoItForReal>
