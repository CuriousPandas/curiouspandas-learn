Imagine a student who memorises the answers to last year's exam, word for word. On last year's paper they score 100%. On this year's paper, with new questions, they're lost. They learned the answers, not the subject.

Models do exactly this. It's called **overfitting**: the model fits its training examples, including their noise and accidents, so closely that it stops working on anything new. The defence is just as simple as the problem. **Hold some examples back.** Never train on them, and judge the model only on how it does with those.

That's what the dots with a white ring have been all along: the **test set**. "Test accuracy" in these widgets is always measured on them, never on the training dots.

## Make it overfit, on purpose

Set this up exactly, then train for about twenty seconds and keep your eye on two numbers:

1. Drag **Noise** up to **0.5**. The two classes now overlap, so some dots sit on the "wrong" side purely by chance.
2. The network is already big: two hidden layers of 12, with every input ticked (265 knobs).
3. Set the **learning rate** to **1** and press **Train**.

<NeuralPlayground dataset="circle" hidden="12,12" features="x,y,x²,y²,xy,sin x,sin y" title="Too many knobs, too much noise" />

Watch **train loss** fall towards zero as the boundary grows tentacles to wrap around individual noisy dots. Now watch **test accuracy**. When we built this lesson, with these exact settings, it peaked around 83% early on and then *fell* to about 72% as training went on. The model got better at the dots it had seen and worse at the ones it hadn't.

<Callout kind="trap">
A falling training loss is not progress if the test score is falling at the same time. If you only ever look at how a model does on its own training data, overfitting is invisible. The model looks better and better right up to the moment it meets reality.
</Callout>

## Three ways back

- **Fewer knobs.** Try `4` in Hidden layers and only `x` and `y`. A smaller model can't memorise the noise, so it's forced to learn the broad shape.
- **Stop early.** Watch the test accuracy and keep the knobs from its best moment, before memorisation set in.
- **More, cleaner data.** Noise is what gets memorised. More examples make each accident matter less.

Now earn it. Keep the noise at **0.3 or more** and get **85% on the held-back dots**. Big networks tend to lose this one; small ones tend to win.

<Callout kind="real">
Real projects usually split data three ways: **training** (turn the knobs), **validation** (choose settings such as network size and when to stop), and **test** (one final, honest score at the end). Even choosing settings by looking at the test score leaks information into the model, a little at a time. Large language models face a subtler version: if benchmark questions leak onto the web and into the training text, a high benchmark score may partly be memory.
</Callout>
