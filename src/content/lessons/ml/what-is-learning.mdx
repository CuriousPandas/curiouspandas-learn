"Machine learning" sounds mysterious. The idea underneath isn't.

A **model** is a function: numbers go in, an answer comes out. Inside are **knobs**, numbers called *weights* that decide what the function does. A model with the wrong knob settings gives wrong answers. **Training** is the process of finding better settings, automatically, from examples.

It takes three ingredients:

1. **Examples with answers.** Here, dots on a map, each labelled *bamboo* (green) or *rocks* (grey).
2. **A loss**: one number that says how wrong the model is across all the examples. Zero would be perfect.
3. **A way to improve.** For each knob, work out which direction of turning would make the loss smaller, then turn every knob a small step that way. Repeat thousands of times.

That third step is **gradient descent**. The *gradient* is the list of "which way, and how steeply" for every knob at once. Calculus computes it exactly, so nobody has to guess. Picture walking downhill in fog: you can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet.

## Watch it learn

The background colour is the model's current opinion of every spot on the map: green means bamboo, grey means rocks, and paler means less sure. Press **Train** and watch the loss fall and the boundary bend into shape. Dots with a white ring are **held back**: the model never trains on them, and they're what the accuracy number is measured on.

Press **New random knobs** a few times before training. Each start draws a different random boundary. Training almost always walks downhill to a good one anyway. That's the surprising part: random starting knobs plus small steps are enough.

## The learning rate

How big is each step? That's the **learning rate**, and it's one of the knobs *you* set, not the model.

- **Too small:** the loss creeps down so slowly you'd think nothing is happening.
- **Bigger:** faster, up to a point.
- **Too large:** each step overshoots the bottom of the valley, so the loss jumps around and can end up worse than where it started.

Try all three above, pressing **New random knobs** before each one. At **0.03** the ring barely changes in the time you'd wait. At **3**, on this small problem, it learns *faster* than the default. At **10**, watch the loss leap and stumble. Where "too large" begins depends on the problem. On big models it starts much lower, which is why picking the learning rate is a real part of the job.

<Callout kind="real">
Real models work exactly this way, just bigger. They have millions or billions of knobs instead of a few dozen, and each step estimates the gradient from a small random batch of examples instead of all of them ("stochastic" gradient descent). They also use cleverer step rules, such as Adam. The loop is the same: measure the loss, compute the gradient, take a small step, repeat.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="trap">
A low loss on the examples you trained on proves much less than it seems. A model can memorise its examples and still be useless on new ones. That's why the accuracy here is measured only on the held-back dots, and it's the whole subject of [Memorising is not learning](/learn/ml/overfitting/).
</Callout>
