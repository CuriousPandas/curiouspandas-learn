The simplest model there is takes its inputs, multiplies each by a knob, adds them up, and squashes the total into a yes/no probability. On a map, whatever knob settings you pick, the boundary it draws between yes and no is always a **straight line**.

Straight lines handle plenty. They fail on a famous puzzle: the **checkerboard**, also called XOR (exclusive or). Bamboo grows in the top-right and bottom-left quarters, rocks in the other two. No single straight line can separate them.

## Hit the wall

This network has **no hidden layer**: just the inputs `x` and `y`, straight to the answer. Train it and watch the accuracy.

<NeuralPlayground challenge="nn-xor-lean" dataset="xor" hidden="" features="x,y" title="A straight line vs. a checkerboard" />

It gets stuck, far short of solved: on this data it settles not much better than a coin flip. A line *could* do a bit better, tilting so that three of the four quarters land on the right side (about 75%). But that line is confidently wrong about the fourth quarter, and the loss punishes confident mistakes hard, so training settles for a nearly flat, unsure answer instead. Either way, no straight line solves it, and no amount of training changes that.

There are two ways out.

## Way one: give it a better input

Tick the **xy** box, the two inputs multiplied together. In the top-right quarter `x` and `y` are both positive, so `xy` is positive. In the bottom-left both are negative, so `xy` is positive again. In the other two quarters `xy` is negative. Measured by `xy`, the checkerboard is a simple yes/no split, and a straight line handles it easily. That's what the challenge above is asking for.

This was how most machine learning worked for decades: humans invented clever inputs ("features") by hand.

## Way two: let it invent its own

Untick `xy` and type `4` into **Hidden layers**. Now the inputs feed four **neurons**. Each one is its own little straight-line model, passing its answer through a bend (the *activation*, `tanh` or ReLU). The output combines those four bent answers. Bent lines combined can draw corners, curves and closed shapes. Train it.

The hidden layer has worked out something that does the job of `xy` without being told. **That is the reason hidden layers exist**: the network learns its own features. Stack more layers and later ones build features from the earlier ones' features, which is what *deep* in "deep learning" means.

<Callout kind="tip">
The bend is essential. Without an activation, stacking layers just multiplies linear functions together, and the result is still one straight line. Every layer would collapse into a single one.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="real">
The XOR wall has real history. A single-layer "perceptron" can't learn it, and that limit was a well-known argument against neural networks for years. Networks with hidden layers became practical to train once backpropagation (computing the gradient for every layer at once) was widely adopted in the 1980s. The language models later in this course are the same idea at enormous scale.
</Callout>
