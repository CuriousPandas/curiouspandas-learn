When you change a Deployment's image, Kubernetes doesn't stop everything and start again. It makes a **new ReplicaSet** for the new pod template and moves pods across a few at a time. That's a **rolling update**, and its whole job is to never leave you with nothing serving.

Two knobs control the pace. **maxSurge** sets how many extra pods may exist during the update, and **maxUnavailable** sets how many may be missing. Both default to **25%**. The docs put it as: at least 75% of the desired pods are up, and at most 125% exist.

The key rule: beyond the maxUnavailable allowance, an old pod is removed only when a new one is **Ready**. The allowance is 25% rounded down: zero for the three replicas below, one for four, two for ten. So if the new version never becomes Ready, at most that 25% of the old version is ever removed. With small replica counts, none is.

## Stuck is not the same as fixed

The Kubernetes docs walk through exactly this case, a mistyped image tag, and say plainly: *the rollout gets stuck*. After `progressDeadlineSeconds` (600 by default) the Deployment reports `ProgressDeadlineExceeded`. Then, again in the docs' words, Kubernetes **takes no action on a stalled Deployment other than to report a status condition**. Nothing rolls back by itself. A person (or a tool like Flagger) has to.

Your options are what you just did: fix the tag and roll forward, or `kubectl rollout undo` to go back to the previous template.

## When the safe default is the wrong one

Rolling updates assume old and new pods can run side by side. A database with one volume can't. The tilt platform's lab 07 reproduces a real incident: a MySQL Deployment on `RollingUpdate`, where the new pod waits forever for a file lock the old pod holds. **733 restarts, two days, zero alerts.** The old pod kept serving the whole time, so everything looked green. For one replica on one ReadWriteOnce volume, `strategy: Recreate`, which stops the old pod first, is the only correct choice.
