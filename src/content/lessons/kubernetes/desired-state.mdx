Most tools take orders: *start this*, *stop that*. Kubernetes takes **promises**. You say "I want three copies of this app running", and that sentence gets stored. Then a set of small programs called **controllers** keep comparing what you asked for with what actually exists, and fix the difference. Forever.

That's the whole idea. The rest of Kubernetes is that loop applied to more kinds of things.

## The chain of command

- A **Deployment** holds your promise: which image, how many copies.
- It creates a **ReplicaSet**, which owns one particular version of the pod template and keeps the right number of pods alive.
- **Pods** are the running copies, one or more containers each.
- The **scheduler** notices pods with no node yet and picks one for each.
- The **kubelet** on each node notices pods assigned to it and starts their containers.

Nobody calls anybody. Each piece watches for work and does its own part. The simulator below runs those same loops every tick.

## Why "desired state" matters

You didn't write "if a pod dies, start another". Nobody did. The controller saw *want 3, have 2* and closed the gap, the same way it would after a node crash, an eviction, or you fat-fingering `kubectl delete`. Recovery isn't a feature someone remembered to add. It falls out of the design.

The same idea leads straight to **GitOps** later in the course. If the desired state lives in git, a controller can keep the cluster matching the repository, and the repository becomes the source of truth.
