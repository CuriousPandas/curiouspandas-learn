How much energy does asking an AI a question use? In August 2025, Google published a measurement from inside its own systems: "the median Gemini Apps text prompt consumes 0.24 Wh of energy", which it called "a figure substantially lower than many public estimates". The same paper lists earlier outside estimates, including one that "a single GPT-3.5 prompt may consume around 3 watt-hours".

Those are more than ten times apart, and that's not necessarily because anyone was careless. One is a company measuring its own system with access nobody outside has, on one model at one date. Others are outsiders working from assumptions about hardware they can't see, about a different model, sometimes years earlier. Neither side can fully check the other's work. Most numbers in this subject are like that, so this lesson keeps asking who measured what, and when.

Underneath all of it is a physical question: does computing have to cost energy at all?

## A joule, and the limit

A **joule** is a unit of energy. A **watt** is one joule per second, so a 60 W bulb uses 60 joules each second. A **watt-hour** is 3,600 joules, which makes that median prompt 864 joules, by Google's figure.

In 1961 Rolf Landauer, working at IBM, asked whether there was a minimum. He looked at what a computer does that can't be run backwards. Take an AND gate. If it outputs 0, you can't say what its inputs were, because three of its four possible inputs give 0. He called that "logical irreversibility", and he argued it has a physical price: it "requires a minimal heat generation, per machine cycle, typically of the order of kT for each irreversible function".

Here `k` is Boltzmann's constant, a fixed conversion between temperature and energy, and `T` is the temperature in **kelvin**, which is Celsius plus 273.15. For resetting a bit, his number was "0.6931 kT per restored bit", where 0.6931 is the natural logarithm of 2. At 300 K, about room temperature:

```python
import math
k = 1.380649e-23    # Boltzmann's constant, in joules per kelvin
print(k * 300 * math.log(2))
```

That's about 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ joules for each bit erased, when that bit could equally well be 0 or 1. A bit already known to hold the value you want needs nothing done to it, as Landauer points out. He also says his reasoning "gives no guarantee that this minimum is in fact achievable". He knew this was tiny. His paper says the amount "is many orders of magnitude smaller than the heat dissipation in any practically conceivable device", and that the point was that the limit is real. In 2012 a team measured it, using a single tiny particle held in a two-sided trap as a one-bit memory, and found the average heat "saturates at the Landauer bound in the limit of long erasure cycles". So it's been seen in the lab, in one small system, when the erasure was done very slowly.

The limit applies to erasing, not to computing as such. Landauer's own paper describes a three-bit gate that computes AND while keeping enough of its input to be reversed. In 1973 Charles Bennett showed that a general computer can be made "logically reversible at every step". So physics doesn't rule out computing at below this limit, in principle, if you never erase anything. Reversible computing remains a research subject: real chips lose far more energy elsewhere, so this limit isn't what's holding them back.

## How far away are real chips?

The best published breakdown that's easy to check is Mark Horowitz's 2014 table, "Rough energy costs for various operations in 45nm 0.9V". 45 nm names an old manufacturing process now, and newer ones cost less per operation, so treat it as a sense of proportion rather than today's price.

| Operation | Energy (Horowitz, 2014) | Times the Landauer limit for its bits |
|---|---|---|
| 32-bit integer add | 0.1 pJ | about 1 million |
| 32-bit floating-point multiply | 3.7 pJ | about 40 million |
| A whole instruction, with that add inside | 70 pJ | about 760 million |
| 64-bit read from main memory (DRAM) | 1.3 to 2.6 nJ | none for the copy itself, and about 7 to 14 billion if you count the 64 bits it overwrites |

A **picojoule** (pJ) is 10⁻¹² joules, and a **nanojoule** (nJ) is 10⁻⁹. The last column counts 32 bits erased for a 32-bit operation. You get 32 if you count 64 bits in and 32 out, and 32 if you count the old value its result overwrites in a register. That's our estimate, and it isn't from Horowitz.

Two things stand out. The add itself is a small part of the instruction: almost everything goes on fetching it, the registers and the control logic. And reading from main memory costs roughly 20 to 40 times as much as that whole instruction. Reading only copies, so the read itself doesn't have to erase anything, although overwriting the old value in a register does. Either way the Landauer minimum is nowhere near the real cost. Horowitz says "Part of this high cost comes from the very energy-inefficient I/O that DRAM systems use", I/O being the link between chips. In this table, going out to main memory is what's expensive, which is another reason caches matter.

For a whole machine, the Green500 list ranks supercomputers by measured efficiency. In June 2026 the top system, KAIROS, reached 73.282 billion floating-point operations each second for each watt. A watt is a joule per second, so the seconds cancel, and that's 73.282 billion operations per joule, or about 13.6 pJ per operation. That's by the list's own measurement rules, which aren't the same as a whole building's bill. If we count each operation as erasing 64 bits, which is our rough assumption, it's still more than 70 million times the limit. (This is the list's standard benchmark, not a general workload.)

So the gap is many millions. Landauer's limit isn't what constrains today's chips. How they're built is.

## Why voltage is the lever

A chip's energy has two main parts. **Dynamic** energy is spent moving charge each time a transistor switches: about `C V²` per cycle, where `C` is the charge-holding capacity of what's switching and `V` is the voltage. **Leakage** is current that flows even when nothing switches, for as long as the power is on.

Look at what that formula says about speed. The clock isn't in it. A job with a fixed number of cycles costs the same dynamic energy whether it runs fast or slow. Voltage is squared, though, so a modest drop pays well. The catch is that transistors switch more slowly at low voltage, so a lower voltage forces a slower clock. Adjusting both as the load changes is called **dynamic voltage and frequency scaling**, or DVFS.

## At the scale of buildings

Those picojoules add up, and here the numbers get less certain. The honest form is a range, with who said it:

| Source, date | What it says |
|---|---|
| IEA, *Energy and AI*, 2025 | Worldwide, data centres used "around 415 terawatt hours (TWh), or about 1.5% of global electricity consumption in 2024". |
| The same report | About 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case. For 2035, "the range of data centre electricity demand across our cases spans from 700 to 1 700 TWh". |
| IEA, *Key Questions on Energy and AI*, April 2026 | Data-centre demand "grew by 17% in 2025", to 485 TWh, and it projects "950 TWh in 2030". |
| Lawrence Berkeley National Lab, 2024 | US data centres: 76 TWh in 2018 and 176 TWh in 2023, "representing 4.4% of total U.S. electricity consumption". For 2028: "roughly 325 and 580 TWh". |

So the IEA's 2035 high case is about 2.4 times its low case. That's before arguing over assumptions, and these reports state that uncertainty themselves. A forecast like this isn't a measurement of the future: it depends on how quickly AI is adopted, how efficient the hardware gets, and whether grids can keep up.

A building also burns energy on things other than computing, cooling above all. **Power usage effectiveness**, or PUE, is the total energy used divided by what reaches the computers, so 1.0 would be perfect. Google reports 1.09 for its fleet in 2025, its own figure for its own sites, and an average like that says little about anyone else's.

Whether energy means carbon depends on what generated it, which varies by place and hour. Google's paper puts its median prompt at 0.03 grams of CO₂ equivalent, a figure that counts its purchases of clean energy, and which depends on how those are accounted for. Changing what the computation is can matter more than any of this: [Ethereum's move away from proof of work](/learn/algorithms/algorithms-and-people/) is the well-known example, with the same caveat about who did the measuring.

Then there's the hardware itself. The Global E-waste Monitor reports "62 billion kg" of electronic waste generated in 2022, with 22.3 per cent of it documented as properly collected and recycled. That covers all electronics, not just computers, and a chip's whole cost includes making it, which none of the joules above count.

<GoDeeper title="Where kT ln 2 comes from">
A bit in thermal equilibrium could be in either of two states, so the collection of possible physical states covers both. Resetting it to a known value cuts the number of states in half. Boltzmann's definition of entropy is `S = k ln W`, where `W` counts the states, so halving `W` reduces entropy by `k ln 2`. Landauer's paper writes this as "k logₑ 2 = 0.6931 k per bit".

The second law of thermodynamics says the entropy of a closed system can't fall, so that entropy has to go somewhere: into the surroundings, as heat of at least `T` times the entropy change, which is `k T ln 2` per bit.

That only applies to information being destroyed. If a gate's output determines its input, nothing is lost. We can count what's lost: with all inputs equally likely, AND takes in 2 bits, and its output has an entropy of about 0.811 bits, so about 1.189 bits are lost per use. XOR that keeps one input, and Toffoli's three-bit gate, lose none, and the lab's test checks these from their truth tables. Real chips don't sit near this: they use many times the thermal energy on purpose, so that thermal noise doesn't flip bits, which is one more thing Landauer's paper considers.
</GoDeeper>

## What to remember

- Landauer's limit is the minimum heat for erasing a bit that could equally be 0 or 1: about 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K, and it has been seen in a lab experiment.
- Real chips are millions of times above it, and their cost is mostly in moving data and control. The limit isn't what constrains them today.
- For a fixed number of cycles, switching energy depends on voltage squared, not on the clock. Slowing the clock alone can cost more.
- Power and energy are different. Always check which one a claim is about.
- Data-centre figures are estimates and scenarios, and they vary widely. Ask who measured what, and when.
