Before a language model can read a single word, the text has to become numbers. The step that does that is the **tokenizer**, and it shapes more of a model's behaviour than most people realise.

Why not just use letters? Too many tiny steps: a page would be thousands of them, and the model would spend its effort spelling. Why not whole words? There are too many words, new ones appear every day (`skibidi`, `GPT-5`, your username), and the model would choke on anything it had never seen.

The answer most modern models use sits in between: **subword tokens**, learned by an algorithm borrowed from data compression called **byte-pair encoding (BPE)**.

1. Start with every single character as its own token.
2. Count every pair of neighbouring tokens in a big pile of text.
3. Glue the most common pair into one new token. `t` + `h` → `th`.
4. Repeat, thousands of times. `th` + `e` → `the`. ` ` + `the` → ` the`.

Common words end up as one token. Rare words get split into familiar pieces. Nothing is ever *unknown*: in the worst case a word falls back to single characters.

## Train one

<Callout kind="tip">
Notice the leading space. ` panda` (with a space) and `panda` are *different tokens*. Look for `·` in the chips. That's why a model can behave differently on the same word at the start of a sentence and in the middle of one.
</Callout>

Try typing things the story never contained: a long made-up word, a number like `1234567`, some code, another language. They break into many small tokens. That's the tokenizer telling you this text was rare in its training data.

<Callout kind="real">
Real tokenizers learn from vastly more text, and most work on **bytes** rather than characters, so every possible input, emoji included, can be encoded. The tiktoken README says that on average, in practice, a token corresponds to about **4 bytes** of text, and that encoding is reversible and lossless. Our toy, trained on one short story, compresses much less. The ratio is in the stats above, so compare.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="trap">
Ask a model to count the letters in a word and it often gets it wrong. Part of the reason is right here: it doesn't see letters. ` strawberry` might reach it as two or three chunks, and the letters inside are something it has to *remember* about those chunks, not something it can look at.
</Callout>
