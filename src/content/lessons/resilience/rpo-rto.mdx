A disk dies, a region goes dark, someone runs the wrong script. However it happens, the morning after comes down to two questions:

1. **How much did we lose?** Everything written since the last good copy is gone.
2. **How long were we down?** From the moment it broke to the moment it worked again.

You can't choose when disaster strikes, but you can choose, in advance, how bad each answer is allowed to be. Those choices have names.

## Two numbers, decided before anything breaks

NIST's contingency planning guide, SP 800-34, defines them:

- **Recovery point objective (RPO):** "the point in time to which data must be recovered after an outage". In practice, the most data you can afford to lose, measured in time. An RPO of 1 hour means you must never lose more than the last hour's work.
- **Recovery time objective (RTO):** "the overall length of time an information system's components can be in the recovery phase before negatively impacting the organization's mission or mission/business processes". In practice, how long you can be down.

Behind both sits a third number from the same guide, **maximum tolerable downtime**: "the amount of time mission/business process can be disrupted without causing significant harm". That's the business's limit. RTO is the target you plan to, so it has to fit inside it.

AWS's disaster recovery whitepaper puts the first half plainly: how often you run your backup "will determine your achievable recovery point". The second half is the same idea: your recovery method determines your achievable recovery time.

## The business sets them, per system

It's tempting for IT to pick numbers that sound responsible ("everything backed up every 15 minutes!"). But the right RPO for online orders and the right RPO for the marketing site are different, because losing an hour of each costs very different amounts. So the targets come from the people who know what an hour of each system is worth, and IT's job is to meet them at the lowest cost.

Try it with three systems. Set a backup schedule and a recovery method, then let the disaster hit.

<Callout kind="trap">
RPO is about the worst case. If the disaster happens to land just after the nightly backup, you lose ten minutes - and nightly backups still can't promise a 1-hour RPO, because the next disaster may land at 01:59. Judge a schedule by the gap it allows, not by the disaster you got.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="real">
In a real outage the clock starts before the restore does. Someone has to notice, decide it is a disaster, find the runbook and the credentials. A restore that takes three hours on a quiet afternoon can take much longer at 03:00 - which is why recovery methods get practised, as the next lessons show.
</Callout>
