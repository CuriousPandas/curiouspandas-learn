Backups protect your data. **Disaster recovery (DR)** is about the rest: if the building, the data centre or the whole cloud region you run in is gone, where does the system run tomorrow, and how quickly can it be there?

AWS's whitepaper *Disaster Recovery of Workloads on AWS* sorts the answers into four approaches, "ranging from the low cost and low complexity of making backups to more complex strategies using multiple active Regions". The names are used across the industry, whichever cloud or data centre you're in.

## What separates them

- **Backup and restore** is, in the whitepaper's words, "a suitable approach for mitigating against data loss or corruption", and for many systems it's enough. Recovery means redeploying "the infrastructure, configuration, and application code" as well as the data. Without infrastructure as code, it warns, that "may be complex" and can blow your RTO.
- **Pilot light vs warm standby** is the distinction people mix up. The whitepaper is precise: "pilot light cannot process requests without additional action taken first, whereas warm standby can handle traffic (at reduced capacity levels) immediately."
- **Multi-site active/active** "is the most complex and costly approach to disaster recovery, but it can reduce your recovery time to near zero for most disasters".

None of them is "best". The right one is the cheapest that meets a system's RTO and RPO, which is why the targets from the previous lesson come first. Most organisations end up with different patterns for different systems.

<Callout kind="trap">
Replication protects against a lost site, not a bad write. A bad deploy that corrupts the orders table is copied to the standby region within seconds, so failing over gives you two corrupt copies. The whitepaper is blunt: continuous replication "may not protect you against data corruption or destruction unless your strategy also includes versioning of stored data or options for point-in-time recovery". Even active/active needs backups, and for a data disaster its recovery point "will always be at some point before the disaster was discovered".
</Callout>

<Callout kind="real">
The figures in the simulator are illustrative, not price quotes: real costs depend on how big the workload is and how much of it runs in the standby site. Whatever the numbers, the whitepaper's other rule holds: "It is critical to regularly assess and test your disaster recovery strategy". A failover nobody has practised is the slowest failover there is.
</Callout>
