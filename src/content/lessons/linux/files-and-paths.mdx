Windows gives every drive a letter: `C:`, `D:`. Linux has no letters. It has **one tree**, and everything hangs from a single root written `/`. Your files, the machine's settings, even the hardware: all of it has a place somewhere under `/`.

Learn that tree and you can find your way around any Linux machine, whether it's your laptop, a server or the container a cluster is running for you.

## Where am I, and how do I get there?

Your shell always stands in one directory, the **working directory**. `pwd` prints it. Paths come in two kinds:

- **Absolute** paths start with `/` and are read from the root: `/etc/hostname`. They mean the same thing wherever you are.
- **Relative** paths don't start with `/` and are read from where you're standing: from `/home/panda`, `projects/bamboo` means `/home/panda/projects/bamboo`.

Two names are in every directory: `.` is the directory itself and `..` is its parent. So `cd ..` climbs one level, and `../../notes.txt` climbs two and then picks a file.

## The files ls doesn't show you

`ls` skips any name that starts with a dot. That's a convention, not a security feature: dot-files are mostly settings (`.bashrc` configures your shell) that would otherwise clutter every listing. `ls -a` shows them, along with `.` and `..`.

## Why "everything is a file"

Linux presents far more than documents as files. **Devices** live under `/dev`. **Running processes** appear under `/proc`, one folder per process ID, with files describing each one. Settings live in `/etc`. Because they're all files, the same handful of tools - `cat`, `echo`, `>` - work on every one of them.

The most famous is `/dev/null`. Anything written to it is thrown away, and reading it gives you nothing at all. It's where you send output you don't want: `noisy-command > /dev/null`.

<Callout kind="real">
The sandbox has the shape of a real system but only a few of its files: no `/proc`, no real devices beyond `/dev/null`, and `ls` prints no colours. A real `ls -l` also shows hard-link counts and dates that come from your clock. On your own machine, `ls /proc` lists a numbered folder for every running process.
</Callout>

<DoItForReal id="linux-paths-real" needs="terminal" xp={20}>

Look at your own machine's tree, its hidden files and its live process files:

```bash
pwd
ls -la ~
cat /etc/hostname
head -5 /proc/$$/status    # Linux and WSL: your own shell, as a file
echo "gone" > /dev/null; cat /dev/null
```

On a Mac there is no `/proc`; `ps -p $$` shows the same shell instead.
